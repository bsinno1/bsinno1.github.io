Research Agenda

I study measurement: whether the instruments social science increasingly relies on — large language models used to score political text — actually measure what they claim to, with what reliability, and under what conditions their scores can legitimately be compared across time and settings. My dissertation develops the Measurement, Evaluation and Validation (MEV) framework, which treats constrained LLM evaluators as scientific instruments whose validity, reliability, and comparability must be demonstrated rather than assumed.

Current work extends that discipline to AI evaluation itself. How much of a measurement claim can be checked depends on how much of the model is visible, so the questions change with the regime: whether a score is valid when only outputs can be seen, whether it is stable when the whole stack is controllable, and whether the internal signal deserves trust when the computation is exposed.

The agenda's aim is a general statistical framework for quantifying evaluator uncertainty across both open-weight and proprietary-model regimes, with reporting standards that let researchers state how much confidence an AI-generated measurement deserves. Substantive applications center on political language over time — partisan affect, ideology, and legislative speech — across comparative and international contexts.

Completed — the dissertation

Measuring Political Concepts Over Time with Constrained Large Language Models
Ph.D., Political Science (Political Methodology), Rutgers University — conferred May 17, 2026
Committee: Beth Leech (chair) · Junyi Jessy Li (Linguistics, UT Austin) · Richard Lau (political psychology) · Jan Kubik (interpretive methods)

The dissertation develops the Measurement, Evaluation, and Validation (MEV) framework for measuring latent political concepts — partisan affect, national economic ideology — in text spanning several decades, where the meaning of the concepts themselves drifts. Constrained large language models (fixed output space with human-in-the-loop validation, temperature-0 inference, era-grounded exemplars) serve as the measurement instruments; the constraints support reproducibility and limit annotation, prompt, and automation bias.

Scores are validated against exogenous benchmarks, and a drift taxonomy (semantic, conceptual, predictive) distinguishes change in a concept from change in the instrument. Analyses cover ANES survey text (1984–2020) and extend to congressional floor speech, with fully seeded, reproducible pipelines.

Current — Measurement Science for AI Evaluation

A validated instrument raises harder questions than it settles, and the dissertation's result is the starting point for them rather than the conclusion.

The program that follows treats the instrument in two ways at once. In one direction it is a tool to be pushed further: automated so that validation runs as part of measurement rather than after it, and carried back into decades of survey text that the feeling thermometer never covered. In the other it is an object to be studied: if the field is going to copy validated judges into smaller open-weight models — as it now routinely does — then whether validity survives the copy is an empirical question, and whether agreement with a validated teacher is even the right evidence is a prior one.

The common thread is that a measurement claim has to be earned. What makes an AI-generated number trustworthy is not the model that produced it but the apparatus around it: a criterion external to the model, a validation that could have failed, and a record showing it was not rewritten after the fact.

How much of that apparatus is available depends on how much of the model you can see. Three regimes, three different questions:

  • Black box Is the score valid? A closed model reached only through its outputs. Nothing inside can be inspected, so the evidence has to come from outside — a criterion the model never saw, checked at the boundary.
  • White box Is the score stable? Open weights and full control of the execution stack. Every knob is settable, so whether identical configuration yields identical output stops being an assumption and becomes a measurement.
  • Glass box Can the internal signal be trusted? The computation itself is visible — per-layer trajectories, attention, the path to an answer. A score certified at the surface still says nothing about how it was arrived at.

The dissertation and its automation work in the black box. Certification of the evaluation stack works in the white and glass boxes. The judge studies cross between them: a black-box teacher copied into white-box students, asking what survives the crossing.

How this work is run — what can an outsider check?

One regime governs every instrument here. Predictions are sealed before any run — eighteen hypotheses under named falsification conditions for the reliability harness, SHA-256 hash-chained across eight successive preregistrations, each version provably containing its predecessor; six fell, and the falsifications are published as the findings. The judge ladder is that discipline in its sharpest form: a sealed single-read design with an air-gapped criterion, mechanical verdicts, and control gates that bind the experimenter before results are seen.

Provenance is read from the machine, not from documentation. Every harness run emits a self-hashing manifest whose condition vector — precision format, kernels, determinism flags, silicon — is read back from live system state on every execution: continuous monitoring, not point-in-time certification. Every ruling lands verbatim in an append-only decision log — the dated, attributed test records EU AI Act Annex IV §2(g) describes, aligned to NIST AI RMF record-keeping.

The discipline is not decorative. The gates bind before results are seen, which means they can and do block my own headline claims — a governance apparatus that has never stopped anything is not evidence of anything. MIT-licensed, CI-verified, and built to be re-derivable by an independent party on commodity hardware: the check a second-line reviewer or external auditor could actually run. Seals, receipts, and decision logs are private during IP review — request access.

Partisan Affect Before the Feeling Thermometer Analysis ongoing

What can be measured in the decades before the criterion existed?

The feeling thermometer — the criterion the dissertation validated against — begins in 1978. The ANES open-ended like/dislike questions run back to 1952. Between those two dates lies a quarter-century of Americans describing the parties in their own words, with no scale to anchor them.

This project extends the affect measure toward 1952. The evidentiary situation changes as it goes back: verbatim response text is published from 1984 onward, while 1952–1980 survives as coded mentions rather than the words themselves. What an instrument validated on verbatim text can legitimately claim over coded mentions is the methodological question the project turns on, and the approach for that span is being worked out rather than assumed. The source archive is secured and the pipeline re-established; analysis is underway.

Testing the instrument

The dissertation validated a GPT-4-based instrument for measuring partisan affect in open-ended ANES text against respondents' own feeling thermometers. These follow-up studies ask what happens when that instrument is copied — as the field now routinely does — into open-weight models.

The first distilled the judge into Qwen and Mistral under hash-chained preregistration and tested the copies against the same held-out criterion: they agreed with GPT-4 at 0.74 but reached only 0.54 validity against respondents, versus the teacher's 0.70 — a copy inherits a fraction of its teacher's grip on the world, not the grip itself.

The second built a 29-judge ladder under run-by-run experimenter-controlled fine-tuning to test whether agreement with a teacher is a sufficient statistic for validity at all — whether the field's standard model-against-model validation is circular.

Validity versus Agreement In progress

Is agreement with a validated teacher a sufficient statistic for validity — or is model-against-model validation circular?

A 29-judge ladder trained run-by-run under experimenter control — two model families, three sizes, three data doses, mid-training snapshots, and a scrambled-label control — spanning the range from close imitation of the teacher to substantial divergence, with each judge scored on both axes: agreement with the teacher, and validity against the held-out human criterion. If agreement were a sufficient statistic for criterion validity, the two would track each other along the ladder.

The apparatus is the point. A sealed single-read design: the criterion is air-gapped, the decision log is append-only, and control gates bind the experimenter before any result is seen. The study is built so the answer can come out either way and be recognised as such — and so that no one, including me, can quietly decide afterwards which way it came out. The judges demonstrate the method; the audit regime is the contribution.

Certifying the Evaluation Stack Reported

Is the score stable, and can the model's internal signal be trusted — before any judge built on it is validated at all?

White box — is the score stable? Weights and configuration in hand, the whole stack is controllable, so reproducibility becomes testable rather than assumed. A purpose-built reliability harness — when the measurement layer is itself the object of study, the instrument cannot inherit untested machinery — testing whether identically configured temperature-0 evaluation ever disagrees with itself, and pricing every condition that makes it disagree. Four open-weight models (Qwen2.5-0.5B/7B, SmolLM2-360M, Mistral-7B), frozen hash-verified prompt suites, five to ten cold-start replications per configuration — 108 hash-verified runs — spanning CPU/GPU execution, precision formats, batching, threading, autotuned kernels, and tensor-parallel inference on pinned NVIDIA A100s, with hypotheses hashed before any run.

Every replication was bit-for-bit identical. Variance appears only between configurations, four orders of magnitude apart with precision format dominant — number format alone moves declared MMLU accuracy by up to four points in either direction, and fp16 silently destroyed 99.5% of one 7B model's token probabilities, perfectly reproducibly, while sparing its equal-sized cross-architecture twin. The bitwise-zero baseline doubles as a tripwire for silent environment change.

Glass box — can the internal signal be trusted? Behaviour certified at the surface says nothing about what the model computed on the way there. A decoding instrument exposing per-layer internals over a single cached forward pass, evaluated on LAMBADA — logit-lens trajectories, per-head attention maps, a temperature-controlled beam tree — subjecting internal signals to the same standard as any score, and gating pass/fail verdicts into the same hashed attestation records as the behavioral certification.
